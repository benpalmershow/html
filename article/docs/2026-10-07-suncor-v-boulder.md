# Suncor Energy (U.S.A.) Inc. v. County Commissioners of Boulder County

**Docket No. 25-170** | **Argued Monday, October 5, 2026, 10:06 a.m.** | **First argument of October Term 2026**

<details>
<summary>Case Overview</summary>

The Court heard the first oral argument of the October Term 2026 in a climate tort case with national implications. Boulder County and the City of Boulder sued Exxon Mobil Corporation and three Suncor Energy companies in Colorado state court in 2018, asserting state-law tort claims - public and private nuisance, trespass, unjust enrichment, and civil conspiracy - for the costs of climate change harms, alleging the companies produced, promoted, refined, marketed, and sold fossil fuels while concealing and misrepresenting the dangers of their use. The energy companies contend the claims are preempted by federal law. The Colorado Supreme Court held they are not, and the companies petitioned for certiorari. The Court granted the petition on February 23, 2026, and added a question on its own statutory and Article III jurisdiction. Justice Alito recused (announced September 28, 2026), leaving eight justices to hear nearly two hours of argument. A decision is expected by summer 2027.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Parties</summary>

- **Petitioners:** Suncor Energy (U.S.A.) Inc., Suncor Energy Sales, Inc., Suncor Energy Inc., and Exxon Mobil Corporation
- **Respondents:** County Commissioners of Boulder County and City of Boulder, et al.
- **Amicus curiae supporting Petitioners:** United States (Sarah M. Harris, Principal Deputy Solicitor General), by leave of the Court with divided argument

</details>

<details>
<summary>Questions Presented</summary>

1. Whether federal law precludes state-law claims seeking relief for injuries allegedly caused by the effects of interstate and international greenhouse-gas emissions on the global climate.
2. (Added by the Court, Feb. 23, 2026) Whether this Court has statutory and Article III jurisdiction to hear this case.

<summary>2. Amendments (Constitutional Provisions Invoked)</summary>

- **Article III:** Whether the Court has jurisdiction, and whether the costs of interim litigation constitute a concrete injury for standing.
- **28 U.S.C. § 1257 (statutory):** Whether the Colorado Supreme Court's C.A.R. 21 order is a final judgment (a "self-contained original proceeding" under the Court's Atlantic Richfield framework) or an ordinary interlocutory appeal.
- **Dormant Commerce Clause / equal sovereignty:** Whether the Constitution's structure bars a state from applying its law to global conduct; the direct-nexus requirement of National Pork Producers.
- **Due Process (extraterritoriality):** Whether a state may extend its law beyond its borders to regulate global conduct with global effects (BMW v. Gore line).
- **Foreign affairs power:** Whether the suit interferes with the nation's conduct of foreign policy.

</details>

<summary>5. Hypotheticals Brought Up</summary>

1. **Shanmugam (rebuttal):** On a visit to Colorado, Boulder could sue him "for refilling my car and thereby contributing to global climate change." Any individual who has suffered climate effects could sue under Boulder's theory.
2. **Thomas to Russell:** Under Boulder's theory, could state and local governments sue "large retailers" of fossil fuels? Russell: "Nothing in our theory prevents that," but multiple defendants can be liable for widespread harm.
3. **Barrett to Russell:** Could Colorado pass a law capping greenhouse-gas emissions in the other 49 states? Russell agreed that would be prohibited and distinguished a damages suit that "requires them to internalize a portion of the cost" rather than prohibiting conduct.
4. **Kagan:** Boulder's suit resembles the 1990s tobacco suits and opioid manufacturer suits - "This is chapter three" - where state claims proceeded.
5. **Harris:** The constructive-presence exception (bringing cars or opioids into a state) makes the actor liable for in-state conduct; Colorado's theory is different - a ban on making the product anywhere nationwide.
6. **Roberts:** It is not uncommon for suits to rest on out-of-state conduct with broad effects felt in the forum state; he asked what makes this different.

<details>
<summary>7. Issues at Hand</summary>

- **Primary:** Whether federal law (the Clean Air Act, displaced federal common law, constitutional structure, or foreign affairs) precludes Boulder's state-law tort claims.
- **Threshold:** Whether the Court has statutory (28 U.S.C. § 1257 finality) and Article III jurisdiction, given the case is still pending in Colorado trial court and other federal defenses remain unresolved.
- **Practical implications:** Some 90,000 municipalities could bring similar suits; potential bankruptcies of energy defendants; a possible remedy gap if neither state nor federal law supplies a cause of action.
- **Possible outcomes:** merits ruling on preemption; dismissal for lack of jurisdiction; 4-4 tie (Alito recused) leaving the Colorado Supreme Court's judgment in place without precedent; dismissal of certiorari as improvidently granted.
- **Stakes:** The fate of Boulder's suit and dozens of similar climate accountability suits nationwide; Gorsuch and Barrett are the key votes to watch.

<summary>Significance</summary>

The most important climate liability case to reach the Court in more than a decade opened the October Term. With Alito recused, a 4-4 split would leave the Colorado Supreme Court's ruling for Boulder in place without binding precedent. The argument revealed a possible majority skepticism toward Boulder's theory on the merits (Roberts, Kavanaugh, with Barrett and Gorsuch interested in a preemption rationale), but also serious doubts about jurisdiction (Thomas, Jackson, Sotomayor), leaving dismissal as a live outcome. The Court could also reach the merits through fractured routes, and the decision will shape dozens of similar suits nationwide.

<details>
<summary>Word Counts & Definitions</summary>

The Court does not publish per-term word counts; the terms below dominated the argument (used repeatedly by all three advocates and across questioning). Exact counts are not tabulated from the transcript.

- **Extraterritorial:** Beyond a state's borders. Petitioners' core claim: Colorado may not apply its law to conduct occurring worldwide with only incidental effects in Colorado. The Court has never recognized a freestanding constitutional "extraterritoriality principle"; Roberts and Kavanaugh probed whether one is needed.
- **Enclave:** An area of exclusive federal authority or an inherently federal domain. Kavanaugh: "This Court has consistently said that air and water pollution are ... matters for federal law" - framing interstate pollution as a federal enclave where state law presumptively does not apply unless Congress preserves it.
- **Preemption:** Federal law superseding state law. Variants argued: express (Clean Air Act text), field (Congress occupied the field), conflict (state law stands as an obstacle), and the newer "foreign affairs preemption."
- **Saving clause:** A statutory provision preserving state law. Harris argued the CAA's saving clause preserves only suits under source-state law (per International Paper Co. v. Ouellette in the Clean Water Act context).
- **Jurisdiction / finality (28 U.S.C. § 1257):** The Court may review state-court judgments only after the state's highest court has spoken finally. Suncor says the C.A.R. 21 order was a "self-contained original proceeding" (Atlantic Richfield framework); Boulder says it is an ordinary interlocutory ruling.
- **Interlocutory:** Intermediate, not final. Boulder's answer to the appeal: other federal defenses remain pending in Colorado trial court, so there is no final judgment to review.
- **Standing (Article III):** Whether Suncor's exposure to interim litigation costs is a concrete injury. Russell: the costs of defending the suit are themselves a constitutionally cognizable injury.
- **Dormant Commerce Clause:** The judicial doctrine that states may not discriminate against, or unduly burden, interstate commerce; National Pork Products' direct-nexus test was invoked as a limiting principle.
- **Amicus curiae:** "Friend of the court." The United States argued for petitioners with divided argument (Harris took the middle segment) - granted Sep. 4, 2026.
- **C.A.R. 21:** Colorado Appellate Rule 21, the original-proceeding writ procedure the Colorado Supreme Court used to decide the preemption question.
- **DIG (dismissed as improvidently granted):** A possible outcome in which the Court declines to decide the case at all.
- **Recusal:** A justice's withdrawal from a case. Alito recused (announced Sep. 28, 2026), leaving eight justices; a 4-4 tie affirms the lower court without precedent.
