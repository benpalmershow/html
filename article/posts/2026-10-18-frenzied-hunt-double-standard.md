
> NYT: Frenzied Hunt by Online Trolls To Identify Cornell’s Jane Doe
> Doc Riter: Frenzied Hunt by NYT Trolls To Identify Cornell’s Joe Suspect. 

The New York Times reports on the frenzied online hunt to identify Cornell's "Jane Doe" in a campus incident. Doc Riter's quip cuts both ways: what about the frenzied hunt by NYT trolls to identify Cornell's "Joe Suspect"?

The double standard is obvious. When amateur internet sleuths try to unmask a victim or piece together details, they're condemned as ghoulish, invasive, or reckless. But when major outlets engage in the same behavior - digging into identities, speculating, or feeding the very attention they're decrying - it gets a more respectable gloss.

The outrage depends on who's doing it. If it's anonymous posters on 4chan or X, it's a moral failing. If it's an investigative desk with a masthead, it's "public interest journalism." But the incentives and consequences look remarkably similar: clicks, attention, and the pressure to publish first.

Doc Riter's point isn't to defend online trolls. It's to point out the asymmetry. Journalistic ethics shouldn't bend depending on whether the keyboard belongs to a credentialed reporter or a random user. The principle - don't gratuitously expose private individuals or fuel mob identification - ought to apply across the board. Otherwise it's just shilling for one's own tribe.
