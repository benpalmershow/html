A recent New York Times front page story frames $10-per-gallon gas as a direct consequence of Trump being president, clearly positioning it as part of the "affordability crisis" angle ahead of the next election. Doc Riter takes a more skeptical read of what the story actually reports.

Buried in the details is a different reality: in that remote location, residents rent a gas generator for $5,000 a month because solar would cost $3 million to install. That single fact reframes the entire headline. The high cost isn't just "gas prices under Trump" so much as the structural economics of energy in a remote, isolated grid.

So the story is less about national inflation politics than about tradeoffs in extreme geography. But the framing leads readers to a predetermined conclusion: blame the president, blame the party in power. The nuance gets lost while the narrative fits neatly into a partisan message.

That's the distinction between reporting facts and crafting a narrative. When a story tells readers they're informed, but probing questions reveal context that undermines the headline, it's no longer straight news. It's advocacy packaged as reporting. And it erodes trust in the process more than any particular price ever could.
