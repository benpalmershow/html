The [New York Fed term premia](https://www.newyorkfed.org/research/data_indicators/term-premia-tabs#/overview) break down Treasury yields into expected future short rates and the term premium (extra compensation for locking up money longer).

**Why current rates are considered low relative to the neutral rate:** 

The "neutral rate" (r*) is the short-term interest rate that neither stimulates nor slows the economy. Right now, many estimates put r* above current inflation. That means real interest rates (rate minus inflation) are still positive - so policy isn't actually that loose. 

Even if nominal rates seem high compared to recent history, they're low relative to where they would need to be if the neutral rate has risen. Higher r* implies that to keep policy neutral (or restrictive), rates may need to stay higher than they were in the low-inflation, low-rate era of the 2010s. The term premium matters too, but the key point is: if the neutral rate is above inflation, current rates may not be as restrictive as they look.
